Research

Doctoral dissertation

Generative AI and Labor Markets: Wages, Informality, and Causal Evidence from Latin America

Third revised version, August 2026 · PhD program in Economics, PUCP
Supervised by Javier Olivera Angulo, Research Scientist at the Luxembourg Institute of Socio-Economic Research and Senior Economist at the National Bank of Belgium

This paper provides the first causal estimates of the effect of large language models on employment and wages outside the OECD: a continuous-treatment difference-in-differences design, estimated country by country on harmonized labor force surveys from eight Latin American countries (2021 to 2025), with the occupational exposure score of Eloundou et al. as the dose. Aggregate effects are heterogeneous and fragile, and employment responds little. The systematic signal is a price: building the outcomes separately for formal and informal workers, exposure lowers the formal monthly wage in six of the eight countries, while informal wages rise in Brazil, Colombia and Ecuador. An individual-level composition test locates the adjustment at the entry margin: the entrant-incumbent wage differential is negative and significant in all five countries with tenure data, and in Peru and Brazil incumbent earnings do not move at all. The formal-informal decomposition, which the high-income economies where the existing causal estimates were produced cannot support because their informal sectors are marginal, is the central contribution.

JEL: J23, J31, O33, O54, D63, L86 · Keywords: generative AI, large language models, labor demand, wage inequality, informality, Latin America, difference-in-differences

Policy research in progress

Ongoing work at the Social Protection and Labor Markets Division of the Inter-American Development Bank. These projects are unpublished; results are not posted here.

Disemployment risk from advances in AI in Latin AmericaIn progress, 2026

Exposure to generative AI is only half of the risk a worker faces; the other half is whether a feasible exit into a less exposed occupation exists at all. This project measures exposure alongside escape capacity across six Latin American countries, using workers linked between consecutive interviews of rotating labor force panels, and asks what role informality plays as a route out.

Employment transitions in Central AmericaIn progress, 2026

Central America's export matrix has shifted towards medical instruments and towards cables and electrical apparatus. This project asks which occupations sustain those dynamic export sectors, what skills they require, and how exposed they are to artificial intelligence, combining employment surveys across the region with online vacancy data from the IDB Labor Observatory.
