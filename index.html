About me

I am an economist with a passion for mathematics and quantitative methods in economics. My work asks what happens to workers when the demand for what they do changes faster than they can move, and what social protection systems owe them when it does.

Most of my research concerns Latin American labor markets, where roughly half of all workers are informal, so the usual margins of adjustment operate on only part of the labor force. My dissertation, supervised by Javier Olivera Angulo, estimates the causal effect of generative artificial intelligence on employment, wages and informality across eight Latin American countries.

I am an economics consultant in the Social Protection and Labor Markets Division of the Inter-American Development Bank, and a part-time professor at the Graduate School of the Peruvian University of Applied Sciences. Earlier I worked at the IDB in Washington, DC, and for seven years at the Central Bank.

Research

Doctoral dissertation

Generative AI and Labor Markets: Wages, Informality, and Causal Evidence from Latin America

Third revised version, August 2026 · PhD program in Economics, PUCP
Supervised by Javier Olivera Angulo, Research Scientist at the Luxembourg Institute of Socio-Economic Research and Senior Economist at the National Bank of Belgium

This paper provides the first causal estimates of the effect of large language models on employment and wages outside the OECD: a continuous-treatment difference-in-differences design, estimated country by country on harmonized labor force surveys from eight Latin American countries (2021 to 2025), with the occupational exposure score of Eloundou et al. as the dose. Aggregate effects are heterogeneous and fragile, and employment responds little. The systematic signal is a price: building the outcomes separately for formal and informal workers, exposure lowers the formal monthly wage in six of the eight countries, while informal wages rise in Brazil, Colombia and Ecuador. An individual-level composition test locates the adjustment at the entry margin: the entrant-incumbent wage differential is negative and significant in all five countries with tenure data, and in Peru and Brazil incumbent earnings do not move at all. The formal-informal decomposition, which the high-income economies where the existing causal estimates were produced cannot support because their informal sectors are marginal, is the central contribution.

JEL: J23, J31, O33, O54, D63, L86 · Keywords: generative AI, large language models, labor demand, wage inequality, informality, Latin America, difference-in-differences

Disemployment risk from advances in AI in Latin America

In progress, 2026

Exposure to generative AI is only half of the risk a worker faces; the other half is whether a feasible exit into a less exposed occupation exists at all. This project measures exposure alongside escape capacity across six Latin American countries, using workers linked between consecutive interviews of rotating labor force panels, and asks what role informality plays as a route out.

Employment transitions in Central America

In progress, 2026

Central America’s export matrix has shifted towards medical instruments and towards cables and electrical apparatus. This project asks which occupations sustain those dynamic export sectors, what skills they require, and how exposed they are to artificial intelligence, combining employment surveys across the region with online vacancy data from the IDB Labor Observatory.

Study notes

Notes I have compiled from textbooks aligned with the purpose of each course, brought into a single self-contained sequence. Each set works from the bibliography listed on its title page and closes every chapter with exam-style exercises.

Foundations of Real Analysis in Economics

Graduate course · 2026 · PDF, 1.5 MB

Starts from the logic of propositions and quantifiers and the proof techniques that go with them, then builds sets, functions and countability, the axioms of the real numbers, the triangle inequality, and supremum, infimum and completeness. From there it moves to the vector space of n dimensions and its norms, their equivalence and the geometry of the balls they induce, and closes with sequences, convergence and continuity in that space.

Worked from Alabiso and Weiss, Moroșanu, Pons, Ok, and Zorich.

Concavity and Optimization in Economics

Graduate course · 2026 · Ten chapters · PDF, 2.5 MB

Runs from the mathematical preliminaries an optimization course assumes all the way to Lagrangian duality. The first half covers real vector spaces, the topology of n dimensional space, differential calculus up to the Hessian and Taylor’s theorem, quadratic forms and definiteness, convex and affine sets, conic structure with the Carathéodory and Minkowski theorems, and hyperplanes and the separation theorems with the Farkas lemma. The second half covers concave and convex functions, generalized concavity and the bordered Hessian, subdifferentiability, unconstrained optimization, the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker theorem with its constraint qualifications and the economic reading of the multipliers, and saddle points and strong duality.

Economic applications are worked in full rather than mentioned: the Second Welfare Theorem proved by separation, risk aversion through Jensen’s inequality, least squares as an orthogonal projection, cost minimization as the dual of production, and a principal agent problem.

Worked from Aragón, Goberna, López and Rodríguez; Boyd and Vandenberghe; Brinkhuis; Li; Nocedal and Wright; Ruszczyński; Snyman and Wilke; and Wright and Recht.
